01Three decades of discovery

A three-decade burst of discovery.

Until 1995 no planet beyond the Solar System had been confirmed around a Sun-like star. Today there are 6,445. Each dot is one world, placed where it sits in Earth’s sky and colored by how it was found. Scroll, and each era’s harvest lights up.

1992

Before the floodgates

Pulsar planets came first. In 1992 Wolszczan and Frail reported two worlds orbiting the millisecond pulsar PSR B1257+12, with a third following in 1994. They were strange and unsettling, and barely felt real. Around normal stars: silence.

Precursor era · 1992–1994 · +3 planets · 3 total
1995

51 Pegasi b

Mayor and Queloz announce a Jupiter-mass world hugging a Sun-like star in a four-day orbit. The first true exoplanet. Radial velocity becomes the dominant detection method and a trickle of hot Jupiters follows.

Radial-velocity dawn · 1995–1999 · +27 planets · 30 total
2000

Transits arrive

HD 209458 b is the first planet caught crossing its star’s face. Ground-based surveys (HAT, WASP, KELT) start grinding out hot Jupiters while the radial-velocity catalog quietly grows.

Transit era opens · 2000–2008 · +288 planets · 318 total
2009

Kepler

NASA’s Kepler mission stares at a single field for four years and changes everything: Earth-sized planets, multi-planet systems, statistics. Most of its harvest is confirmed in bulk a few years later.

Kepler prime · 2009–2013 · +593 planets · 911 total
2014

The Kepler flood

Statistical validation releases hundreds of Kepler planets at once in 2014 and again in 2016. After its reaction-wheel failure Kepler is reborn as K2, surveying the ecliptic, while microlensing (KMTNet, OGLE) reaches into the galactic bulge.

Validation and K2 · 2014–2017 · +2,683 planets · 3,594 total
2018

TESS goes live

The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite scans nearly the entire sky for nearby, bright stars whose planets can actually be studied. Catalog growth accelerates again.

TESS all-sky · 2018–2021 · +1,300 planets · 4,894 total
2022

JWST and the present

The James Webb Space Telescope starts probing atmospheres. Direct imaging matures. Ground-based radial velocity joins TESS for follow-up, and the archive keeps growing.

Current frontier · 2022–2026 · +1,551 planets · 6,445 total

03TESS

Eight years of TESS.

05Sources and method

Where the data comes from.

Everything on this page is drawn from one query to the NASA Exoplanet Archive (Caltech/IPAC, operated for NASA under the Exoplanet Exploration Program). The query, the fetch date and the counts are recorded in data/meta.json, and the page’s captions are computed from the data at build time rather than typed.

The query

Planetary Systems table, default parameter set only, so each planet appears once with the archive’s preferred values:

select pl_name,hostname,disc_year,discoverymethod,disc_facility,pl_orbper,pl_rade,pl_bmasse,pl_eqt,pl_insol,pl_orbsmax,pl_orbeccen,st_spectype,st_teff,st_rad,st_mass,ra,dec,sy_dist,sy_pnum,releasedate from ps where default_flag=1

Fetched 14 October 2026; the newest release date among the rows is 8 October 2026. Re-running fetch_data.py refreshes the atlas.

Reading the page

The sky map uses equatorial coordinates (right ascension 0–360°, declination −90° to +90°). The system browser spaces orbits on a log scale of semi-major axis (estimated from period and stellar mass where the axis is not published) and compresses extreme period ratios so slow outer planets still move.

Planet classes in the system browser follow radius: below 1.6 R⊕ rocky, 1.6–4 sub-Neptune, 4–8 Neptune-like, above 8 giant. The habitable band on the insolation chart spans 0.32 to 1.55 times Earth’s flux, a common optimistic range.
